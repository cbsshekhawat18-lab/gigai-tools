The old routine for signing a PDF was absurd: print the page, sign it in pen, dig out a scanner or photograph it with your phone, crop the result, and email it back, losing quality at every step. You don't need any of that. If you can open a PDF in a browser, you can type your details, drop in a signature, and send a clean finished file back in about a minute. No printer, no scanner, no app to install.

This guide walks through the whole thing, and then answers the question most guides skip: **when is a drawn signature actually enough, and when do you need a certificate-based digital signature?** Those are two different things, and knowing the difference saves you from over- or under-doing it.

## What you need before you start

Almost nothing:

- The PDF you've been sent.
- A device with a modern browser, phone, tablet or computer all work.
- Optionally, a photo of your handwritten signature (we'll cover how to make a clean one).

That's it. Because the whole process runs in your browser, the document never has to be uploaded anywhere.

## Step by step: sign a PDF in your browser

Open your file in the free [PDF editor](/pdf-editor) and work through the page.

### 1. Fill in the text fields first

Most forms that need a signature also need your name, a date, an address or a reference number. Don't handwrite these, type them. Choose the **text** tool, click where the value belongs, and type. A few habits keep typed text looking like part of the original page rather than something bolted on:

- **Match the font size** to the printed text around it. Oversized type is a giveaway.
- **Sit text on the line**, not floating above it, nudge the box down to the baseline.
- **Use black** for a formal document. Blue reads as "signed in pen" if you prefer that feel.

### 2. Add your initials where the form asks

Multi-page contracts often want your initials on every page, not just a full signature at the end. The fastest way is to type your initials with the text tool, or draw a small version once and reuse it. Place them wherever the little "___/___" markers appear.

### 3. Add the signature itself

You have two honest options here, and both are legitimate:

- **Draw it.** Switch to the **pen** tool and sign directly on the page. On a phone or tablet with your finger or a stylus you can get a genuinely good signature. On a laptop trackpad it takes a couple of tries, and there's an undo if a stroke wanders.
- **Stamp an image of your signature.** If you already have a picture of your signature, use the image/stamp tool, then drag a box on the signature line to size and place it. This gives the most consistent result across every document you ever sign.

### 4. Export the finished PDF

When everything's in place, export. Your text, initials and signature flatten onto the page and a normal PDF downloads, ready to email or upload.

## Make a signature image that doesn't look terrible

If you go the stamp route, the quality of your signature image is everything. The single biggest mistake is using a **JPG**, because a JPG always carries a solid white rectangle that will sit over the form line like a sticker.

Do this instead:

1. Sign a blank sheet of white paper in dark ink.
2. Photograph or scan it in good light.
3. Remove the white background so it becomes transparent, and save it as a **PNG**.
4. Aim for roughly 600 pixels wide so it stays crisp when scaled down.

A clean transparent PNG can be reused forever. Save it somewhere handy and you'll never rebuild it.

## Drawn signature vs digital certificate: which do you actually need?

This is where most "how to sign a PDF" advice goes quiet, so let's be clear. There are two genuinely different things people mean by "signing":

| | Drawn / typed signature (e-signature) | Digital signature (certificate-based) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | An image of your mark placed on the page | A cryptographic seal tied to a verified identity |
| Proves | That someone put a signature there | *Who* signed and that the file is unaltered since |
| Setup needed | None | A digital certificate from a trusted authority |
| Good for | Consent forms, approvals, quotes, internal sign-offs, most everyday agreements | Legal filings, regulated industries, high-value contracts where tamper-evidence is required |
| Cost | Free | Usually paid, via a certificate provider |

For the vast majority of everyday documents: a permission slip, a rental application, an approval, a freelance agreement, an offer letter you're accepting. A **drawn or typed signature is perfectly acceptable.** Many jurisdictions treat a clear intent to sign as legally binding regardless of whether ink or pixels made the mark. That's the kind of signing this browser workflow is built for, and it's what the overwhelming majority of people need.

You step up to a **certificate-based digital signature** only when a specific process demands it: certain government filings, notarized documents, or regulated financial and legal work where the recipient must be able to *verify* your identity and prove the file hasn't changed since you signed. That requires a certificate issued to you by a trusted authority. Something a free in-browser editor can't manufacture, and shouldn't pretend to. If a form explicitly asks for a "digital certificate" or "qualified electronic signature," that's your signal to use a dedicated certificate service rather than a drawn mark.

The honest summary: use the free drawn signature for everyday agreements, and reserve certificate-based signing for the rare document that specifically requires verified identity.

## Signing more than one page or file

Real paperwork is rarely a single tidy PDF.

- **Several files to sign as one packet?** Combine them first with [Merge PDF](/merge-pdf), then sign the single merged document so everything travels together in the right order. A cover letter, a contract and an appendix become one clean file to sign and return.
- **A page needs correcting before you sign?** Fix structure (deleting, reordering or rotating pages) *before* adding your signature, so nothing shifts underneath your mark afterward.

## Protect the signed file before you send it

Once a document carries your signature, it's more sensitive than it was blank. If you're emailing a signed contract or a form with personal details, add a password so only the intended recipient can open it. Run the finished file through [PDF password protect](/pdf-password-protect), set a password, and share that password with the recipient through a different channel: a text or a call, not the same email. It's a 20-second step that keeps a signed agreement from being read by anyone who intercepts or is accidentally forwarded the message.

## Why doing this in the browser matters

A signature and a filled-in form are about as personal as documents get. That's exactly why it's worth doing in a tool that runs entirely on your own device. The [PDF editor](/pdf-editor) processes everything locally in your browser, your document and your signature image are never uploaded, never copied to a server, and nothing is stored. Close the tab and it's gone. For anything with your signature on it, that privacy isn't a nice-to-have. It's the whole point.

## What the export does with your signature

When you place a signature in the [PDF editor](/pdf-editor) and export, the signature image is drawn into the page content on your device: the downloaded file carries it as part of the page itself, not as a detachable annotation someone could lift off in another editor. Your signature image, which is biometric-adjacent data worth guarding, never uploads anywhere in that flow. Draw it, place it, export, and the only copies in existence are on your machine.
